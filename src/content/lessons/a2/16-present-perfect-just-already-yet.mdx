## News: something has happened

You know the form from <LessonRef>lesson 15</LessonRef>, Present perfect: experience: **have / has + past participle**. Here is its second big use: **news** and **recent actions** that have a **result now**. We don't say **when** it happened. We care about the situation **now**.

<Timeline items={[{ type: "arrow", from: -0.35, to: 0, label: "past action → result now" }]} caption="It happened before now, and we can see or feel the result now." />

<Example context="home" fa="وای! کلیدهایم را گم کرده‌ام. (فعلا کلید ندارم.)">Oh no! I<mark>'ve lost</mark> my keys. (I don't have them now.)</Example>
<Example context="health" fa="انگشتم را بریده‌ام. چسب زخم داری؟">I<mark>'ve cut</mark> my finger. Have you got a plaster?</Example>
<Example context="news" fa="قیمت نان بالا رفته است.">The price of bread <mark>has gone up</mark>.</Example>
<Example context="social" fa="شنیدی؟ احمد کار جدیدی پیدا کرده!">Have you heard? Ahmad <mark>has found</mark> a new job!</Example>
<Example context="home" fa="ببین! باران بند آمده.">Look! It<mark>'s stopped</mark> raining.</Example>

Compare the two tenses:

- *I**'ve lost** my keys.* → They are still lost. (result now)
- *I **lost** my keys last week, but I found them.* → A finished story. (past simple)

### News first, details in the past simple

We often give the **news** with the present perfect, and then the **details** (when, where, how) with the **past simple**, because the details belong to a finished time.

<Example context="health" fa="احمد تصادف کرده. امروز صبح از دوچرخه افتاد.">Ahmad<mark>'s had</mark> an accident. He <mark>fell</mark> off his bike this morning.</Example>
<Example context="news" fa="تیم ما جام را برده! دیشب ۲ بر ۱ برد.">Our team <mark>has won</mark> the cup! They <mark>won</mark> 2–1 last night.</Example>

## just: a moment ago

**just** means *a very short time ago*. It goes **between have / has and the past participle**.

<Example context="travel" fa="ببخشید، بس همین فعلا رفت.">Sorry, the bus <mark>has just left</mark>.</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="تازه گزارشم را تمام کرده‌ام.">I<mark>'ve just finished</mark> my report.</Example>
<Example context="social" fa="خواهرم تازه بچه‌دار شده!">My sister <mark>has just had</mark> a baby!</Example>
<Example context="travel" fa="همین فعلا رسیدی؟">Have you <mark>just arrived</mark>?</Example>

The order is fixed: *I've **just** finished*, not ~~I just have finished~~. (*just* can also mean *only*: *It's **just** a game.* That comes in <LessonRef>lesson 43</LessonRef>, already, yet, still, just; also, too, either.)

## already: before now, sooner than expected

**already** means *before now*. It often shows that something happened **sooner than expected**, or that there is **no need** to do it again. It usually goes **between have / has and the past participle**, or at the **end** of the sentence.

<Example context="shopping" fa="«پولش را بدهم؟» «لازم نیست، قبلاً پرداخت کرده‌ام.»">“Shall I pay?” “No need. I<mark>'ve already paid</mark>.”</Example>
<Example context="home" fa="این فیلم را قبلاً دیده‌ایم. یک فیلم دیگر بگذاریم؟">We<mark>'ve already seen</mark> this film. Shall we watch a different one?</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="به این زودی تمامش کرده! خیلی سریع است.">She<mark>'s finished</mark> it <mark>already</mark>! She's so fast.</Example>

In a **question**, *already* shows **surprise**: *Have you finished **already**?* (= That was quick!)

## yet: up to now, in negatives and questions

**yet** means *up to now*. We use it when we **expect** something to happen. It goes at the **end** of **negatives** and **questions**.

<Example context="school" fa="«تکلیفت را انجام داده‌ای؟» «نه، هنوز نه. بعد از شام انجامش می‌دهم.»">“Have you done your homework <mark>yet</mark>?” “No, not <mark>yet</mark>. I'll do it after dinner.”</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="هنوز جوابی از آنها نگرفته‌ام.">I <mark>haven't</mark> heard from them <mark>yet</mark>.</Example>
<Example context="travel" fa="بس هنوز نیامده؟">Has the bus come <mark>yet</mark>?</Example>

- The short answer is **Not yet.**
- Don't use *yet* (with this meaning) in a positive sentence: say *I've **already** finished*, not ~~I've finished yet~~.
- Don't put *yet* at the start: *I haven't finished **yet***, not ~~I yet haven't finished~~.
- *still* in negatives (*The bus **still** hasn't come*) is in <LessonRef>lesson 43</LessonRef>, already, yet, still, just; also, too, either.

## just, already, yet: the summary

<FormTable caption="just, already, yet with the present perfect">

| Word | Meaning | Type of sentence | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **just** | a very short time ago | positive, questions | have + **just** + past participle | I've **just** eaten. |
| **already** | before now, sooner than expected | positive, questions (surprise) | have + **already** + past participle, or at the end | I've **already** eaten. · Have you eaten **already**? |
| **yet** | up to now (we expect it) | negatives, questions | at the end | I haven't eaten **yet**. · Have you eaten **yet**? |

</FormTable>

## No finished time

The present perfect is about **now**, so it doesn't go with a **finished time** (*yesterday, last week, in 2020, two days ago*). With those words, use the **past simple**.

<Example context="home" fa="دیروز کلیدهایم را گم کردم.">I <mark>lost</mark> my keys yesterday.</Example>
<Example context="travel" fa="بس پنج دقیقه پیش رفت.">The bus <mark>left</mark> five minutes ago.</Example>

Not ~~I've lost my keys yesterday~~ and not ~~The bus has left five minutes ago~~. But without the finished time, the present perfect is fine: *I've lost my keys. The bus has just left.*

The full comparison of the two tenses is in <LessonRef>B1 lesson 6</LessonRef>, Present perfect or past simple? The finer choices between them for news and stories come much later, in <LessonRef>C2 lesson 4</LessonRef>, Tense & aspect for stance.

<UkNote>This lesson shows British English. In **American English**, people often use the **past simple** with *just, already* and *yet*, especially in speech: *I **just ate**. Did you **finish** yet? I **already paid**.* British speakers usually say *I've just eaten. Have you finished yet? I've already paid.* Both are understood everywhere.</UkNote>

- I've lost my keys! (کلیدهایم را گم کرده‌ام! یعنی الان کلید ندارم.)
- Our team has won! (تیم ما برده است!)
- اول خبر با ماضی نقلی، بعد جزئیات با گذشته‌ی ساده: Ahmad's had an accident. He fell off his bike this morning. (احمد تصادف کرده. امروز صبح از دوچرخه افتاد.)

**just** یعنی «همین الان / تازه». بین have و شکل سوم فعل می‌آید: I've just arrived. (تازه رسیده‌ام.) The bus has just left. (اتوبوس همین الان رفت.)

**already** یعنی «قبلاً / به این زودی». نشان می‌دهد کاری زودتر از انتظار انجام شده یا لازم نیست دوباره انجامش دهیم. بین have و شکل سوم، یا آخر جمله: I've already paid. (پول را داده‌ام.) در سؤال تعجب را نشان می‌دهد: Have you finished already? (به این زودی تمام کردی؟)

**yet** یعنی «تا الان / هنوز». فقط در جمله‌ی **منفی** و **سؤالی** و همیشه **آخر جمله**: I haven't finished yet. (هنوز تمام نکرده‌ام.) Have you finished yet? (تمام کرده‌ای؟) جواب کوتاه: Not yet. (هنوز نه.)

**با زمان تمام‌شده نه:** با yesterday، last week، ago و مانند این‌ها گذشته‌ی ساده بیاورید: I lost my keys yesterday.

**چرا فارسی‌زبان‌ها اشتباه می‌کنند؟**

- در فارسی «هنوز» اول جمله یا قبل از فعل می‌آید: «هنوز تمام نکرده‌ام». فارسی‌زبان می‌گوید I yet haven't finished. در انگلیسی yet آخر جمله است: I haven't finished **yet**.
- «هنوز» در فارسی هم معنی yet دارد و هم still. برای منفی با معنی «تا الان» از yet استفاده کنید (I haven't eaten yet). still در درس ۴۳ می‌آید.
- در فارسی برای «همین الان رفت» گذشته‌ی ساده می‌گوییم. در انگلیسی بریتانیایی با just معمولاً ماضی نقلی می‌آید: The bus **has just left**. (در انگلیسی آمریکایی The bus just left هم رایج است.)
- در فارسی «دیروز کلیدم را گم کرده‌ام» را هم گاهی می‌گوییم. در انگلیسی با yesterday فقط گذشته‌ی ساده: I **lost** my keys yesterday.

<MemoryHook title="Just in the middle, yet at the end">
**just** and **already** sit in the **middle**, between *have* and the past participle: *I've **just** / **already** eaten.* **yet** waits at the **end**, and only in **negatives** and **questions**: *I haven't eaten **yet**. Have you eaten **yet**?*
