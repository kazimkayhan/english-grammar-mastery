## have and has

<FormTable caption="have: present">

| | Positive | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they | I **have** a car. | I **don't have** a car. | **Do** you **have** a car? |
| he / she / it | She **has** a car. | She **doesn't have** a car. | **Does** she **have** a car? |

</FormTable>

Short answers: *Yes, I **do**. / No, she **doesn't**.*

## What do we use have for?

<Example context="home" fa="دو خواهر و یک برادر دارم.">I <mark>have</mark> two sisters and a brother.</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="او یک دفتر بزرگ دارد.">She <mark>has</mark> a big office.</Example>
<Example context="health" fa="امروز سرم درد می‌کند.">I <mark>have</mark> a headache today.</Example>
<Example context="shopping" fa="کیسه داری؟">Do you <mark>have</mark> a bag?</Example>

We also use **have** in fixed expressions for **actions**:

- **have** breakfast / lunch / dinner
- **have** a shower / a bath
- **have** a good time / a party / a break

<Example context="social" fa="بیا با هم ناهار بخوریم.">Let's <mark>have</mark> lunch together.</Example>

## have got

**have got** means the same as **have** (possession, family, illness). It's common in British English and in speech:

- *I've got a car.* = *I have a car.*
- *Have you got a pen?* = *Do you have a pen?*
- *She hasn't got time.* = *She doesn't have time.*

Don't use **have got** for actions: *I have breakfast* (not *I've got breakfast*).

<UkNote>British speakers often say **have got**: *I've got two brothers. Have you got a minute?* Americans usually say **have** (*Do you have a minute?*).</UkNote>
