## Every word has a job

Grammar is easier when you know what each word **does**. These jobs are called **parts of speech** (or **word classes**). English has nine.

<FormTable caption="The nine parts of speech">

| Part of speech | Its job | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun | names a person, place, thing or idea | Zahra, Herat, phone, love |
| verb | shows an action or a state | go, eat, be, have |
| adjective | describes a noun or a pronoun | big, hot, happy |
| adverb | describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb: how, when, where, how much | slowly, very, today, here |
| pronoun | takes the place of a noun | I, you, he, she, it |
| preposition | comes before a noun to show place, time or direction | in, on, at, to |
| conjunction | joins words or parts of a sentence | and, but, or, because |
| determiner | comes before a noun and shows which one or how many | a, an, the, my, this, some |
| interjection | shows a sudden feeling | Oh! Wow! Ouch! |

</FormTable>

<Example context="home" fa="مادرم چای داغ می‌نوشد." note="determiner · noun · verb · adjective · noun">My mother drinks hot tea.</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="او خیلی با دقت کار می‌کند." note="pronoun · verb · adverb · adverb">She works very carefully.</Example>
<Example context="travel" fa="ما در کابل و هرات هستیم." note="pronoun · verb · preposition · noun · conjunction · noun">We are in Kabul and Herat.</Example>

### Nouns and verbs

- A **noun** names a person (*Zahra, a teacher*), a place (*Afghanistan, school*), a thing (*coffee, a phone*) or an idea (*love, happiness*).
- A **verb** shows an action (*eat, go, buy*) or a state (*be, have, like*). Every English sentence needs a verb.
- Some verbs are **helpers**. They work with a main verb: *I **can** swim.* You will meet them in <LessonRef>lesson 21, “can / can't (and how modal verbs work)”</LessonRef>, and at level <LessonRef>A2, lesson 14 (Verb forms & auxiliary verbs)</LessonRef>.

Nouns for things have no gender in English. We use the same *a*, *the* and adjectives for every noun: *a big man, a big woman, a big table*. (For people we use *he* and *she*. That is <LessonRef>lesson 4</LessonRef>.)

### Adjectives and adverbs

- An **adjective** describes a noun or a pronoun. It goes **before** the noun (*a **hot** day*) or **after** *be* (*The day is **hot**.*). More in <LessonRef>lesson 29 (Adjectives: before nouns and after be)</LessonRef>.
- An **adverb** describes a verb (*She drives **slowly**.*), an adjective (*It's **very** hot.*) or another adverb (*She drives **very** slowly.*). Many adverbs are an adjective + **-ly**: *slow → slowly, careful → carefully*.
- The adverb of **good** is **well**: *She is a **good** singer. She sings **well**.* More adverbs at level <LessonRef>A2, lesson 41 (Adverbs of manner: quickly, well)</LessonRef>.

<Example context="social" fa="او خواننده‌ی خوبی است. خوب آواز می‌خواند." note="good = adjective · well = adverb">She's a <mark>good</mark> singer. She sings <mark>well</mark>.</Example>

### The small grammar words

- A **pronoun** takes the place of a noun: *Zahra is late. **She** is on the bus.* (<LessonRef>lesson 4</LessonRef>)
- A **preposition** comes before a noun: *in Kabul, at work, to school*. (<LessonRef>lessons 31 and 32</LessonRef>)
- A **conjunction** joins words or parts of a sentence: *tea **and** coffee · I'm tired, **but** I'm happy.* (<LessonRef>lesson 33</LessonRef>)
- A **determiner** comes before a noun: *a phone, the phone, my phone, this phone*. (<LessonRef>lessons 9, 10, 11 and 12</LessonRef>)

When a noun has a determiner **and** an adjective, the determiner comes first: **determiner + adjective + noun**.

<Example context="shopping" fa="این گوشی جدید من است." note="determiner · adjective · noun">This is <mark>my new phone</mark>.</Example>

### Content words and grammar words

Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs carry most of the **meaning**. We call them **content words**. This group is open: new words come into English all the time.

Pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions and determiners are **grammar words**. This group is small and closed: new ones almost never appear. They are short, but they hold the sentence together.

### One word, two jobs

Many English words can do more than one job. The **place** in the sentence shows the job.

- After a subject, *drink* is a verb: *I **drink** tea.* After *a*, it is a noun: *Can I have a **drink**?*
- *I **work** in a bank.* (verb) · *I go to **work** at eight.* (noun)
- *She sings **well**.* (adverb) · *I'm **well**, thanks.* (adjective: healthy) · ***Well**, I'm not sure.* (interjection)

<Example context="social" fa="می‌شود یک نوشیدنی بگیرم؟" note="a + drink → noun">Can I have a <mark>drink</mark>, please?</Example>
<Example context="health" fa="خوبم، ممنون." note="well = healthy → adjective">I'm <mark>well</mark>, thanks.</Example>

## The parts of a sentence

A sentence has **parts**, too. Each part answers a question.

<FormTable caption="Sentence parts">

| Part | Its job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| subject | who or what the sentence is about; it comes before the verb | **Zahra** buys bread. |
| verb | what the subject does or is | Zahra **buys** bread. |
| object | the person or thing the action happens to; it comes after the verb | Zahra buys **bread**. |
| complement | comes after *be* and describes or names the subject | Zahra is **tired**. Zahra is **a doctor**. |
| adverbial | where, when, how or how often | Zahra buys bread **every morning**. |

</FormTable>

Every English sentence needs a **subject** and a **verb**. The other parts depend on the verb. <LessonRef>Lesson 3 (Every sentence needs a subject: SVO order)</LessonRef> shows the word order in detail.

<Example context="shopping" fa="زهرا هر روز صبح نان می‌خرد." note="subject · verb · object · adverbial">Zahra buys bread every morning.</Example>
<Example context="work" fa="رئیسم امروز خیلی سرش شلوغ است." note="subject · verb · complement · adverbial">My boss is very busy today.</Example>

### A part can be a group of words

A sentence part is not always one word. A group of words can be one part.

<Example context="home" fa="برادر کوچکم هر شب شیر داغ می‌نوشد." note="subject (3 words) · verb · object (2 words) · adverbial (2 words)"><mark>My little brother</mark> drinks <mark>hot milk</mark> <mark>every night</mark>.</Example>

Ask the question: *Who drinks hot milk?* **My little brother.** That whole group is the subject.

### Verbs with and without an object

- Some verbs **need** an object: *like, want, need, buy*. Say *I like **it**.*, not ~~I like.~~
- Some verbs **never** take an object: *sleep, arrive, come*. *The baby sleeps.* *Our bus arrives at six.*
- Many verbs can do both: *We eat at seven.* / *We eat **rice**.*
- A few verbs need an adverbial: *She lives **in Bamyan**.* (*She lives.* is not complete.)

<Example context="travel" fa="اتوبوس ما ساعت شش می‌رسد." note="subject · verb · adverbial (no object)">Our bus <mark>arrives</mark> at six.</Example>
<Example context="shopping" fa="«این پیراهن را دوست داری؟» «آره، دوستش دارم.»" note="like + object">“Do you like this shirt?” “Yes, I like <mark>it</mark>.”</Example>

### Object or complement?

An **object** is a different person or thing: *Zahra **meets** a doctor.* (Zahra and the doctor are two people.)

A **complement** comes after *be* and describes the subject: *Zahra **is** a doctor.* (Zahra = the doctor.) A complement can be a noun (*a doctor*) or an adjective (*tired*). After *be*, use an **adjective**, not an adverb: *She is **happy**.*, not ~~She is happily.~~

### Adverbials

An **adverbial** tells us **where**, **when**, **how** or **how often**. It can be one word (*here, today, slowly*) or a group of words (*at home, every morning, by bus*). It often comes at the end: *We study English **at home**.*

## Interjections

An **interjection** is a short word that shows a sudden **feeling**: surprise, pain, happiness. It usually stands alone or comes at the start of a sentence. It is not part of the subject or the verb, and its form never changes.

<FormTable caption="Common interjections">

| Interjection | Feeling | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Oh** | surprise, a strong feeling | Oh, I didn't know that. · Oh no! Where are my keys? |
| **Wow** | something surprising or great | Wow! Look at that! |
| **Ouch** | sudden pain | Ouch! That hurts! |
| **Oops** | a small mistake, a little fall | Oops! Sorry! |
| **Hooray** | great happiness | Hooray! No school tomorrow! |
| **Hey** | to get attention; an informal hello | Hey, wait a minute! · Hey, what's up? |

</FormTable>

After an interjection we usually write an exclamation mark (**!**) for a strong feeling, or a comma (**,**) when the sentence goes on: *Oh, I see.*

<Example context="health" fa="آخ! درد می‌کند!" note="interjection">Ouch! That hurts!</Example>
<Example context="school" fa="هورا! فردا مدرسه تعطیل است!" note="interjection">Hooray! No school tomorrow!</Example>
<Example context="social" fa="وای! آپارتمان جدیدت عالی است!" note="interjection · determiner · adjective · noun · verb · adjective">Wow! Your new apartment is great!</Example>

Some other small words work in the same way. They stand alone and are very common in speech:

- *yes, no, OK*: answers
- *hi, hello, bye, thanks*: greetings and polite words
- *um* (British English also *er*): a sound you make when you stop to think: *Um, I'm not sure.*
- some adjectives, alone: *“I passed my exam!” “**Great!**”*

Most interjections are **informal**. Use them when you speak or send messages, not in formal writing.

<MemoryHook title="Name it, do it, describe it">
**Nouns name** things. **Verbs do** things. **Adjectives describe nouns**, **adverbs describe verbs** (and adjectives). The small words are the **glue**. And when you feel something suddenly, say it with an **interjection**: *Wow!*
</MemoryHook>
